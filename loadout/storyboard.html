Loadout

How product moves through the shop, from approved proposal to installed, and what gets recorded at each step

Version 0.12.2, updated Nov 13, 2026 What changed

Read this first

Loadout is a web app for the office and the warehouse, used on a phone in the rack room and on a desktop at the front. It turns an approved Portal proposal into a job and follows every part on it from ordered to installed. It keeps a running count of what is on the shelves, including how many feet are left in each wire box. Every change is written to a log with the name of the person who made it, so any count can be explained later. It is built and in use; this document now describes the app as it is, and the open questions that remain are marked.

Every client, name, number, price, and address on this page is invented sample data. The buttons drawn inside the pictures are pictures. What you can press on this page: the seven tabs above, the contents links below, each comic panel's title (it opens that step in the Storyboard), the SOP link under each storyboard panel, the role sheet picker, and the print buttons at the bottom.

Overview

The office makes a job from the client's proposal, the warehouse pulls it from the shelves into a bay, and a tech picks it up and delivers it to the client. What the shelves cannot cover is ordered and received into the same bay, and closing the job sends what was not used back to the shelf. Every move carries a name and a time, so any count can be explained later.

The rest is the five-minute read. The In pictures tab has the comic and this chart full size, the Storyboard tab shows the screens, and the SOP tab has the step-by-step procedures. Print this tab on its own with the button at the bottom.

The problem it solves

A proposal is approved in Portal, product gets bought, and boxes land at the shop. Before Loadout it was memory and sticky notes from there: which boxes belong to which job, what is on the shelf, how many feet are left in a wire box, and who has the spare access point in their van. Loadout puts one record behind each of those questions, and a scan with a phone is how you answer them.

How a job moves through it

The comic on the In pictures tab tells the job in nine panels, and the Storyboard shows each screen. A job is what used to be called a ticket; its lines are the pull sheet. A carrier's box at the shop has Arrived; Deliver only ever means the client's site. What the comic leaves out:

  • New job. Use a proposal file (.csv) reads the file Portal exports and marks each line On the shelf list, New to Loadout, or Kept as a note. Make the list yourself is for a service call with no proposal.
  • Pull. The Pull screen lists each line by shelf in walking order with one button, such as Pull 2; the QR on the printed pull sheet opens it. On the job page, Pull everything in stock fills what the shelves can cover, and Show on board puts the job on the TV.
  • Order and receive. Order missing puts every Short line on a draft PO: Mark ordered, then Add shipment with the tracking number. Office, Orders and tracking shows every package and the install Week. A unit at or above $250 gets its own QR and asks for its serial or MAC at Receive. A box shipped to a job site gets Mark arrived instead and never touches the shelves.
  • Wire. After pulling cable, scan the box, type the footage mark into Mark now, and press Save cut; the box's feet left update.
  • Close. Close job waits for anything still in a van. Each leftover in the bay goes back to stock or is written off with a reason, and units still out become Installed at the job. Copy for Portal copies a status note to paste into the proposal.

What it keeps track of

  • Products and where they sit. Every product has a shelf, a count, an optional photo, and a barcode learned the first time it is scanned.
  • Wire boxes. Feet left in every box, and every cut: how much, by whom, for which job.
  • Serialized units. Anything at or above the QR threshold gets its own label, named like "Ubiquiti UDM-SE #1". One scan shows where it is, who has it, and its history; a service call years later can scan the label in the rack and see when it went in.
  • Jobs. One per approved proposal, with its install date and its pull sheet: needed, from stock, to order, staged in the bay, picked up, delivered.
  • Orders and shipments. Vendor, order number, where it ships, carrier, tracking number, expected date, and every status change with who made it and when.
  • A log of every change. Every movement (receive, pull, move, stage, pickup, deliver, cut, reading, adjust, checkout, checkin, return, writeoff, note, edit) carries a name and a time. Nothing changes anonymously.

The screens

Five places, the same on the phone's tab bar and the desktop's menu: Home, Jobs, Scan (the raised button in the middle), Shelf, and Office (More for techs). A tech's sections there are Labels, Movements, This phone, and About; the rest of this document says Office.

The header, drawn at the top of every desktop sketch: the Loadout mark on the left (press it to go Home from anywhere), the menu, and on the right one account block, the sync pill (Synced, Offline, or "3 pending") with your name beside it and your role under them in grey. On a phone it is the pill with the role under it. Press the block for the Account sheet: your name, email, role, one sentence on what the role can do, and Sign out. The footer shows the version only.

  • Home: the doors (Pick up from staging, Confirm a delivery, Pull for a job, Receive a shipment, Audit a shelf) over the open jobs. Each door says how much waits behind it. Each door has its color, and the same color runs as a thin band along the top of that door's screens.
  • Jobs: every job by status, with filters, an Install column, a progress strip, and one next-step button; open one to see its pull sheet by area.
  • Scan: the camera and whatever the scan found: a product card, a unit card, a wire box's box sheet, or a shelf.
  • Shelf: the catalog with All, Low, Out with people (who has which unit), and Locations. Filters for Category, Type, Brand, and Location, plus Consumables only, narrow All and Low; each option shows its count, the active ones show as chips under the search, and on a phone they sit behind one Filter button. A product with serialized units lists them in place, and Show history on any product, unit, or location reads as a timeline, newest first. A product opens in place under its row; its Brand and Category are picked from a list that narrows as you type and shows how many products carry each, so one brand is never saved three ways. Only an admin adds a new one, after a confirm that shows the name with its capitals fixed; anyone else is told to ask an admin.
  • Office: Orders and tracking (with Week), Clients, Reports, People, Settings, Audit, Labels, Movements, Import inventory sheet, Backups, Export everything, and About. Each person sees the sections their role can use.

The wall board has no menu: the TV by the bays, read-only and live, a grid of up to six jobs. Opened on a phone it becomes one scrolling column of the same jobs.

Portal

Loadout works with Portal through the proposal CSV that Portal already exports, on any Portal plan, and Copy for Portal hands a status note back to paste in. On the Premium plan, Zapier can push an accepted proposal into Loadout on its own: the worker that receives it is built, and a job it makes carries an "Imported from Portal, review" chip until someone presses Reviewed. Portal's full API, which would let Loadout write its note onto the proposal by itself, is only on the Unlimited plan after an application to Portal.

What we need from you

The app runs on assumed defaults for these. Each answer is a small change:

  1. Which Portal plan the company is on, so we know whether the Zapier import and automatic notes can be turned on.
  2. Who is in each role: admin, warehouse, tech, accounting; and whether techs may receive product.
  3. One real proposal CSV from Portal, so the import is checked against the real export.
  4. Wire box start lengths: keep the defaults by cable type (1,000 ft for Cat5e, Cat6, and coax; 500 ft for audio, speaker, and stranded) or type each open box's start once.
  5. Assigned inventory: whether there is a per-job list beside the Unassigned Inventory sheet, and whether it should come in as staged stock.
  6. What will drive the TV by the bays: a small PC, a streaming stick, or a laptop.
  7. Turn on Firebase Storage for the project and deploy the storage rules, so product photos can be saved.
  8. A service account for the worker with two roles, Cloud Datastore User and Storage Object Admin, so the nightly backup and the Zapier import can go live. No Drive folder is needed.

What it is not

Not a replacement for Portal or for QuickBooks, no automatic carrier tracking yet, and no unit-by-unit tracking of cheap stock unless you ask for it. The full list is at the end of the SOP tab.

Part 3.What Loadout is not

So expectations match what is built.

  • Not a replacement for Portal. Proposals, pricing, and client approvals stay in Portal. Loadout reads the proposal's CSV and hands a status note back.
  • Not accounting software. No invoices and no bills; QuickBooks stays where it is. Loadout's reports are a check against it.
  • No automatic package tracking yet. Carrier status is typed in or comes from the shipment dates. Checking UPS and FedEx on a schedule is a later add-on that would write into the same status log.
  • No barcode until it is scanned once. Portal does not carry UPCs, so the catalog learns each one the first time a box is scanned.
  • No unit-by-unit tracking below the threshold. Cheap stock is counted, not followed, unless someone marks that product Serialize.
  • It records mistakes; it does not block them. Anyone with the right role can change stock. Every change carries a name, so a wrong count can be traced and fixed.

Tech spec

What Loadout runs on, what each piece costs, and a straight answer to the question "will the data still be readable without the app".

In one paragraph

Loadout is a web page, not an app from an app store. It lives on the company's existing work site, opens in any browser, and installs to a phone's home screen like an app. People sign in with their company Google account and nothing else. Will the data stay readable?

The worry is a fair one. A Firestore database can turn into a pile of documents with random names, fields nested five deep, numbers encoded as codes, and the real meaning spread across a dozen places, so that only the app can make sense of it. That is a design choice, not something Firestore forces, and Loadout is built the other way. The rules below are followed by the app and checked before each release.

  • Flat tables with plain names. One collection per kind of thing: products, locations, jobs, orders, shipments, items, wire boxes, movements, people. Each one is the same shape as a spreadsheet tab, and each maps one for one to a table if the data ever moves to SQL.
  • Readable fields, in English. brand, model, qtyNeeded, ftLeft, holderName. No abbreviations, no codes to decode, no field called x1.
  • Names travel with the record. A movement row says "Ubiquiti U6-Pro, from Rack A3 to Bay 2, by Warehouse, for job 1042" in words. The ID numbers are there too, but a person can read the row on its own.
  • Readable IDs where the world already has one. A job is filed under its Portal proposal number and a purchase order under its PO number. Random IDs are used only where nothing better exists, and the readable fields sit right next to them.
  • No deep nesting and no blobs. At most one level: a job has its lines under it, and so does a purchase order. Nothing is stored as an encoded lump.
  • A written data dictionary. Every collection and every field is listed, with its meaning, in the plan and on the About tab in Office, which reads it from the app's own code so the two cannot drift apart. Someone reading the raw database has the key.
  • A copy outside the database. Export everything gives one CSV per collection (or one workbook) that opens in Excel, and the nightly backup will do the same every day once the worker is live. If Loadout vanished tomorrow, the spreadsheets would still say what is on the shelves and who has what.
  • Dashboards read the same records. The Office's reports are built on the same rows, not on a separate hidden copy, so what accounting sees is what is stored.

Security and access

  • Company Google accounts only. No passwords to manage, no shared logins.
  • Roles are enforced by the database's own rules, not only by hiding buttons: accounting and the wall board physically cannot write, and a tech can only change the fields a scan touches.
  • The movement log cannot be edited or deleted by anyone, including admins. Mistakes are corrected by a new entry with a reason.
  • Photos follow the same roles: admin and warehouse add or remove them, everyone signed in can see them, and each file must be a small image.
  • Everything is served over HTTPS. There is no analytics or tracking script on the page.
  • The page carries a public Firebase key by design; it is locked to the company's site addresses so it works nowhere else.
  • Export everything is the backup today, and the nightly backup takes over once the worker is deployed: a folder in the same Google project the database lives in, listed inside Loadout under Office > Backups. Only admin and accounting can open those files, and nobody can change them from the app. Google keeps the database itself replicated.

Costs

  • Hosting and sign-in: no cost, already in place.
  • Database and photos: within the free tier at this size; a few dollars a month if it grows past it.
  • The worker: Cloudflare's free tier covers one nightly run and the Zapier pushes.
  • Portal automation: only if wanted, and only what Portal charges for its Zapier add-on or Unlimited plan.
  • Hardware: label sheets for an office printer, and a small PC or stick for the TV by the bays.
